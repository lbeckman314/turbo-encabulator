Building a Turboencabulator

November 19, 2026 #three.js #anime.js #shaders #physics
Heads up

Nothing on this page measures inverse reactive current. The machine's behaviour is invented to fit the datasheet's descriptions, the way a prop department would. The rendering and the physics, on the other hand, are real, and that's mostly what this post is about.

The source material

The text is older than the sheet. It was written by a British engineering student, John Hellins Quick, and published in 1944 in the student journal of the Institution of Electrical Engineers; Time reprinted it in 1946, and it has been passed around (and performed, straight-faced, on film) ever since. The version I worked from is GE handbook sheet HBK-8359, dated December 31, 1962, which dresses the joke up as a real product listing.

Here's the text as it's usually passed around. The part names link to the machine: hover one to light it up, or click it to jump up and see it. Clicking a part on the machine brings you back here.

For a number of years now, work has been proceeding in order to bring perfection to the crudely conceived idea of a transmission that would not only supply inverse reactive current for use in unilateral phase detractors, but would also be capable of automatically synchronizing cardinal grammeters. Such an instrument is the turbo encabulator.

Now basically the only new principle involved is that instead of power being generated by the relative motion of conductors and fluxes, it is produced by the modial interaction of magneto-reluctance and capacitive diractance.

The original machine had a base plate of pre-famulated amulite surmounted by a malleable logarithmic casing in such a way that the two spurving bearings were in a direct line with the panametric fan. The latter consisted simply of six hydrocoptic marzlevanes, so fitted to the ambifacient lunar waneshaft that side fumbling was effectively prevented.

The main winding was of the normal lotus-o-delta type placed in panendermic semi-boloid slots of the stator, every seventh conductor being connected by a non-reversible tremie pipe to the differential girdle spring on the “up” end of the grammeters.

The turbo-encabulator has now reached a high level of development, and it’s being successfully used in the operation of novertrunnions. Moreover, whenever a forescent skor motion is required, it may also be employed in conjunction with a drawn reciprocation dingle arm, to reduce sinusoidal repleneration.

That last detail sets the terms of the project. There are no blueprints, so everything is reverse-engineered from Fig. 1, a single grainy photo, and the sheet's descriptions of parts that don't exist. The photo turns out to be enough. The machine is a handful of simple solids:

Each part is a THREE.Group built in its assembled position, plus two extra numbers: an explode vector (where it goes when the machine comes apart) and an anchor (where its label's leader line attaches). Assembled is always the origin, so exploding is just tweening each group's position to its vector.

Drawing it like a comic

The look comes from Raj Shekhar's Voyager, which renders the spacecraft like a page from a technical comic: a few flat colours, heavy ink outlines and shading done with halftone dots. Three tricks get you most of the way there.

1 · Three tones, two of them dots

Each surface gets one flat palette colour. The fragment shader compares the surface normal with a light direction: lit faces keep the flat colour, faces turning away get a screen of slightly darker dots, and faces in shadow get ink dots. The dots are laid out in screen space on a 45° grid, as in print, so they stay the same size however close you zoom. Try it:

float l = dot(n, uLight);
vec3 c = uBase;
if (l < 0.3)   c = mix(uBase, uBase * 0.7, screen(r));    // mid-tone dots
if (l < -0.12) c = mix(uBase * 0.78, uInk, screen(0.38)); // ink dots

The light is fixed relative to the camera, not the world, so the shading reads like a drawing as you orbit, rather than like an object turning under a lamp.

2 · Ink outlines from an inverted hull

Every mesh gets a twin drawn with only its back faces, pushed outwards so its rim peeks out around the silhouette as a dark line. The push happens in the vertex shader, in pixels, so the line keeps the same weight at any distance. Hard-edged geometry like a cylinder's cap has split normals that would crack the hull at the corners, so each vertex is pushed along the average normal of every vertex at the same position.

vec4 clip = projectionMatrix * modelViewMatrix * vec4(pos, 1.0);
vec4 nc   = projectionMatrix * modelViewMatrix * vec4(pos + outNormal * 0.01, 1.0);
vec2 d = (nc.xy / nc.w - clip.xy / clip.w) * uView;            // normal, in pixels
clip.xy += normalize(d) * uLine * 2.0 / uView * clip.w;          // push uLine px out

3 · Fade through the dots

Parts don't fade in with transparency; they dissolve through the same dot screen, each dot shrinking from nothing. It's one line in the fragment shader, and it keeps the intro in the printed-page idiom.

Choreography with anime.js

anime.js is usually pointed at DOM elements, but it will animate any plain JavaScript object, which means it can drive a 3D scene directly: mesh.position, a shader uniform's value, or a state object the render loop reads. The whole machine is one such object, and engaging it is a timeline:

createTimeline()
  .add(lamps, { g: [0, 1], delay: stagger(160) }, 0)          // lamps light in turn
  .add(S, { inrush: 1, ease: 'outExpo' }, 520)                // inrush spike…
  .add(S, { inrush: 0.62, ease: 'outElastic(1, .35)' }, 940)  // …settles with a wobble
  .add(S, { rpm: 1, duration: 2800 }, 600)                    // fan spins up
  .add(S, { realization: 0.874, ease: 'outQuart' }, 1100);

stagger() does the rest: parts assemble one after another in the intro, the 47 brushes drop in with a bounce, and labels pop in down the list when you explode the machine.

Forty-seven brushes on a string

At first the brushes simply bobbed in a travelling sine wave, which looked nice and meant nothing. Now each brush is a mass tied to its neighbours, which makes the row a discretized vibrating string:

üi = c² (ui−1 − 2ui + ui+1) − γ u̇i − k ui

u is each brush's height, c the wave speed (about 30 brushes a second), γ the damping (the Viscosity slider) and k a weak pull back to rest. The ends are imaginary brushes: leave one at zero and the string is pinned there, so waves reflect; move one to a waveform and that end is driven. That's all the wave-shape selector does. Integration is semi-implicit Euler at 240 steps a second, which keeps c·h well under 1, the limit past which the string explodes. Here's the same string, flat:

Grabbing a brush in 3D needed one more trick. The brushes are a few pixels wide, so instead of asking the ray to hit one, the code finds where the pointer's ray passes closest to the whole row of brushes, and accepts anything within a brush-width or so (as long as the casing isn't in the way). Dragging then happens on a vertical plane facing the camera, so up always means up.

Wave shapes have consequences

The datasheet has opinions about waveforms, so the selector does more than reshape the drive:

Shape What happens
Sinusoidal Nominal. Realization settles at 87.4%.
Cosinusoidal Drives from the opposite end. The sheet says cosine-wave directors must feed a Phase Inverter first, so until you fit one the drive is garbled, the amber lamp blinks and realization collapses to 31%.
Tangential Every asymptote pegs the inrush needle, rattles the tremie pipes and flashes the red lamp.
Pipusoidal The pentametric fan surges on each pip, and the machine shudders with it.

On film

The text has been performed on camera several times, always with a straight face. Bud Haggart made the first film around 1977 and reprised the role for Rockwell and Chrysler; Mike Kraft took over for Rockwell's Retro Encabulator in 1997 and SANS's HyperEncabulator in 2022.
